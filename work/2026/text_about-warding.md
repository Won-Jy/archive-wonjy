When I had my first Zoom meeting with the Teatergalleriet team for this roughly six-week residency in Småland, Sweden, I asked them what they thought was the most remarkable feature of Kalmar's<sup><a class="note-ref" href="#fn1" id="fnref1">[1]</a></sup> landscape. Their first answer was: "We have a spot where you can see the city prison really clearly."

When I arrived in Kalmar, I could see it right away — the prison, fenced in and standing proudly on an island with sharp, angular corners. It sat in what could literally be called the city center, between the old town and the new town.

This topological position of the Kalmar prison led me, quite naturally, to recall the Hôtel-Dieu<sup><a class="note-ref" href="#fn2" id="fnref2">[2]</a></sup> at the very heart of Paris, on the Île de la Cité. Each is a place that was once both hospital and prison, yet the distinction between those functions grows more blurred the further back in time one goes. All that matters, in the end, is that we set <i>them</i> apart from <i>us</i>. But why is the place where we set them apart our own navel?

In two different cities — Kalmar and Paris — these places lie at the center of the map; what is it that they stir within the landscape of our minds? Are we caring for those we have pushed inside that wall, or those we have pushed <i>outside</i> the "wall"? Or are we surveilling them — through that transparent glass wall?

These were the first questions guiding the research for my residency.

As it happened, the official name of the Swedish prison service was "Kriminalvården."

In the mid-17th century, to defend itself against foreign invasion, the inland city "collectively relocated" to a nearby island and fortified it, raising high walls along the island's edge.

On the ravelin island — which became the principal passage linking the mainland and the island — outside the city walls, they decided to build a prison to hold lawbreakers<sup><a class="note-ref" href="#fn3" id="fnref3">[3]</a></sup>. Less than a century later, with the prison at its center, the mainland opposite the island, where the old city had once stood, was redeveloped, and somehow this prison ended up "wedged" between today's old town and new town, belonging to neither.

Exclusions overlapped; the excluded came to occupy the center, and it radiates its own presence, unfailingly, to everyone who lives here.

In the late 19th century, the moats surrounding the island were filled in, so that it was no longer an island — yet it holds its ground as firmly as the angular corners of the medieval fortress walls, hinting that the place is still an island. Where the moat was filled, there is now a lawn, and in one corner a small playground has been laid out.

One day, not long after arriving in Kalmar, as I walked into town, I saw a child and a family enjoying a peaceful picnic at the foot of that prison wall. They were close enough that an inmate jumping from the building would have landed right on top of them.

As the residency went on, while I lived and worked in Kalmar I had to travel each day to the Pukeberg atelier in Nybro — about thirty minutes by bus each way.

This commute let me watch the region's rural scenery and terrain pass by through the bus window, like a broken panorama returning to its beginning again and again: no matter what detail I missed on a given day, the same scenery would roll past when I rode the bus the next.

Among the landscapes I saw this way, the first thing to catch my eye on the residency's opening day was the walls built of <i>pierre sèche</i><sup><a class="note-ref" href="#fn4" id="fnref4">[4]</a></sup>. The moment I saw them, I could tell they were very different from the dry-stone structures I knew in southern France — few of the lines are straight; they break off from one another midway, or wander off to the side. In other words, you can easily tell they were built organically rather than to a plan.

According to a text I read later, granite boulders split by glacial force and left "slid" away lie scattered like shrapnel throughout the ground here. To the first humans who tried to settle this land, they must have been like landmines in the earth, for they took up the very places where crops were meant to grow.

One day those humans decided to gather on the land. They lined up in a row and advanced, prying up these stones and pushing them aside. I came to imagine the pushed stones forming a mound at the moment that advance halted — whether from exhaustion or for whatever other reason. The stone wall born this way became the starting point of a marker dividing your land from mine, and it also shaped the paths that ran through. This line is like the thin "wall of water" at the edge of a windshield wiper sweeping its fan — there and not there — exerting a lasting hold on the memory through which we look at the world! (I can't tell you how much that water-stain on the glass bothered me!)

Some of these endearing granite rocks poke their heads up out of the ground, and the ones we see today are those that survived the humans' sweeping. As it turned out, those busy humans had nothing to plant there anyway; the stones were too heavy to pull out; and even if they could be, the cost couldn't be ignored — at any rate, whatever the reason, these coincidences piled up and the stones came to remain throughout this human settlement. Yet simply by "being there," they exert an unintended influence: trees and grass grow up beside them, and they also vex us, forcing our paths to curve around them. Conversely, we come to look at them, to take the time to "appreciate" them. At some point the stone comes to be perceived as an ornament evoking a pastoral mood, and so it is folded into the landscape as one of this place's own.

Watching the scenery from the bus, drifting through one thought and another, before I know it I've arrived in Nybro. Getting off and walking to Pukeberg, I have to pass through a typical Swedish housing development. So each day I couldn't help but unintentionally steal glimpses of the way of life here: among the uniformly arranged wooden houses of identical height, every single one had a lawn garden. In some gardens — spaces that felt like a kind of "showroom" for the homeowner — you could see granite stones set into the ground, and granite stone walls (but fixed with mortar), reproduced in imitation.

Wandering through this development in Nybro, the thought that comes to me is that it faintly recalls the residential zones so often seen in American-style urban planning: that the "things one is properly expected to have" within an agreed-upon way of life can be confirmed openly, right there on the surface (building materials, the floor area and number of stories, the size of the yard, garden ornaments, the distance from neighboring houses, and so on).

This standardization of life-on-display implicitly betrays the traces of invisible administrative paperwork and urban planning.

On a map that slices through three-dimensional terrain and landscape as if inlaid, exposing their cross-section, we distinguish and divide zones and the parcels within them — and in that process, little squares are produced.

These little squares are like the cavities of a mold, into which the liquid-like human body is poured to fill them. And even the soul, too.

Why is it that I feel the square of a parcel — the square of a cell — is, conversely, like the walls with which our soul imprisons its own body?

In the late 18th century, in discussing human punishment, it is a curious coincidence that Mably said: let the penalty strike the soul rather than the body<sup><a class="note-ref" href="#fn5" id="fnref5">[5]</a></sup>.

My residency was, in this sense, a kind of process of re-applying and blending the formal language I had developed in France onto the newly given natural-topographical and socio-geopolitical context of Småland and Kalmar.

Karst limestone cliffs gave way to exposed granite bedrock surfacing from the earth; the stonemasons cutting limestone became glassblowers melting and blowing quartz glass; and the concrete fragments I used to gather at Pissevin in Nîmes were replaced by cullet, the waste-glass fragments at the Pukeberg glass school. Yet the "invisible wall" — of the administrative language of modern society's official forms, and of otherness — remains just as universal.

The body and soul that the prison intake form asks after; the festival of contorted punishment and the submission to it. Having gathered these deepened questions, I returned again to France — carrying with me the remembered smell of the pine forest I had wandered through in search of granite stones.

**Won Jy**

June 2026

(function(){
  var notesMap = {};
  document.querySelectorAll('.notes li[id^="fn"]').forEach(function(li){
    var html = li.innerHTML.replace(/^\s*<a[^>]*class="note-num"[\s\S]*?<\/a>\s*/, '').trim();
    notesMap[li.id] = html;
  });

  var tooltip = document.getElementById('fnTooltip');
  if (!tooltip) return;
  var tipAnchor = null;
  var isMobileQuery = window.matchMedia('(max-width: 768px)');

  function placeTooltipNear(el){
    var margin = 8;
    tooltip.style.left = '-9999px';
    tooltip.style.top = '-9999px';
    var vw = document.documentElement.clientWidth;
    var vh = document.documentElement.clientHeight;
    var r = el.getBoundingClientRect();
    var tr = tooltip.getBoundingClientRect();
    var left = r.left + (r.width - tr.width) / 2;
    var top = r.bottom + margin;
    if (top + tr.height + margin > vh) top = r.top - tr.height - margin;
    if (left < margin) left = margin;
    if (left + tr.width + margin > vw) left = vw - tr.width - margin;
    var isFixed = getComputedStyle(tooltip).position === 'fixed';
    tooltip.style.left = (isFixed ? left : left + window.scrollX) + 'px';
    tooltip.style.top = (isFixed ? top : top + window.scrollY) + 'px';
  }

  function showTip(el, html){
    tipAnchor = el;
    tooltip.innerHTML = html;
    tooltip.style.display = 'block';
    requestAnimationFrame(function(){ placeTooltipNear(el); });
  }

  function hideTip(){
    tooltip.style.display = 'none';
    tooltip.innerHTML = '';
    tipAnchor = null;
  }

  window.addEventListener('scroll', function(){
    if (tooltip.style.display === 'none' || !tipAnchor) return;
    if (getComputedStyle(tooltip).position !== 'fixed') return;
    var r = tipAnchor.getBoundingClientRect();
    if (r.bottom < 0 || r.top > window.innerHeight) { hideTip(); return; }
    placeTooltipNear(tipAnchor);
  }, { passive: true });

  window.addEventListener('resize', function(){
    if (tooltip.style.display !== 'none' && tipAnchor) placeTooltipNear(tipAnchor);
  });

  document.querySelectorAll('a.note-ref').forEach(function(a){
    var href = a.getAttribute('href');
    var key = href ? href.slice(1) : '';
    var html = notesMap[key];
    if (!html) return;

    a.addEventListener('mouseenter', function(){
      if (!isMobileQuery.matches) showTip(a, html);
    });
    a.addEventListener('mouseleave', function(){
      if (!isMobileQuery.matches) hideTip();
    });

    a.addEventListener('click', function(e){
      if (!isMobileQuery.matches) return; // desktop: default anchor jump + hover already shown
      e.preventDefault();
      var opened = a.dataset.peek === '1';
      if (!opened){
        a.dataset.peek = '1';
        showTip(a, html);
        var outside = function(evt){
          if (!a.contains(evt.target) && !tooltip.contains(evt.target)){
            a.dataset.peek = '0';
            hideTip();
            document.removeEventListener('click', outside, true);
          }
        };
        document.addEventListener('click', outside, true);
      } else {
        a.dataset.peek = '0';
        hideTip();
        location.hash = '#' + key;
      }
    }, { passive: false });
  });
})();
